# The Money Line: a stroll through the history of money

Status: the first slice is built and `wip` (open it with `?wip=moneyline`): the scene, the handcar between
stops, stations 3 (shells), 4 (Yap) and 5 (first coins) with their moments, coins, golden bananas and eggs, and the
station on Jekyll Island that leads in and back. Everything else below is still to build. This is the source the
scene's `LORE` is written from, so every line a relic says should be checked here first.

## The route

One walk from before coins to the live chain, across four places:

| Stretch | Years | Where | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before money to the gold standard | prehistory to 1900 | **The Money Line**, its own scene, ridden by handcar from a station on Jekyll Island | new |
| Fiat | 1910 to 1971 (and its failures, 1923 and 2009) | **Jekyll Island**, off the south rim | built |
| The cypherpunks | 1989 to 2008 | **a small islet** between Jekyll Island and the Mempool island, bridged to both | new |
| Bitcoin | 2009 to now | the genesis stone, the **Mempool island** (the live chain), the **Lightning Factory** | built |

The Money Line leaves from Jekyll Island and ends by rolling back onto it in 1910, so the scene holds the deep
past and the hub holds the rest. The genesis stone's "way out" becomes a real path: over the cypherpunk islet to
the Mempool island.

## Rules for the words

- **The documented record first, the memes on top.** A tooltip names the relic; the pokes tell its history,
  one fact a line, and the last line may joke. The joke is about money, never about a person.
- **Dates as the sources give them.** Where historians disagree, the line says "about" or "the story goes".
- **No myths stated as fact.** See [Myths we do not repeat](#myths-we-do-not-repeat).
- **Short quotes only**, in quotation marks, with who said them.
- **Every station has one date carved large**, for the puzzle (see [The dates puzzle](#the-dates-puzzle)).

## Stations on the Money Line

Each station is a diorama beside the track: its own ground, light and sky, three to five relics to poke, one
thing to do, and one coin to pick up. The handcar stops at each; Space gets off and on.

### 1. Before money: gift and credit

*Setting:* a village of huts round a fire at dusk. *Carved date:* none (before writing).

- **The favour tree** (tooltip: "Owed favours · the first ledger was memory")
  - Economists long taught that people bartered first and invented money to fix barter's problems.
  - Anthropologists looked and did not find it: Caroline Humphrey's survey found no barter economy "pure and
    simple" anywhere. Neighbours gave, owed and remembered.
  - Barter does happen, mostly between strangers, or where money has broken down.
- **Gift pile** (tooltip: "A gift · you owe Ooga one now")
  - Ooga gave you a banana. Nobody wrote it down. Everybody knows.

*The moment:* trade a favour with a villager Ooga; the debt shows as a knot on a cord that stays tied until you
pay it back with a poke.

*Coin:* a knotted cord.

### 2. Clay and silver: Mesopotamia

*Setting:* mud-brick temple storehouse, barley sacks, a scribe's bench. *Carved date:* **1754 BCE** (the usual
date for the Code of Hammurabi).

- **Clay tablets** (tooltip: "Clay ledger · debts in silver")
  - The earliest written records are mostly accounts: grain in, grain out, who owes what.
  - Values were reckoned in silver by weight, the shekel, or in measures of barley. A shekel was a weight,
    not a coin.
  - The Code of Hammurabi set a measure (gur) of barley equal to a shekel of silver.
- **Weights and scale** (tooltip: "Shekel weights · 60 to the mina")
  - 60 shekels made a mina, 60 minas a talent. Weights still varied from town to town.
- **Barley sacks** (tooltip: "Barley · money you can eat")
  - Silver was easier to carry: a gur of grain was a load for donkeys.

*The moment:* press a tally into wet clay; the tablet dries and joins the shelf.

*Coin:* a sliver of hacksilver.

### 3. Shells and beads

*Setting:* a beach of white sand, cowries in the shallows, a longhouse's wampum belts at the treeline. *Carved
date:* **1200 BCE** (cowries in Shang China; given as "about").

- **Cowrie strings** (tooltip: "Cowries · small, shiny, hard to fake")
  - Cowrie shells were valued, strung and buried in Shang-dynasty China; copies in bone and stone were made
    when real shells ran short.
  - The old character for cowrie, 貝, sits inside characters for wealth, goods, costly and poor.
  - Cowries served as money across Africa and Asia for centuries.
- **Wampum belt** (tooltip: "Wampum · shell beads on the American coast")
  - Wampum beads were woven by peoples of the northeastern coast. Colonists started using them as money.
  - Massachusetts made wampum legal tender in 1637: six white beads to the penny, small sums only.
  - Over-production and counterfeits followed, and the law was repealed in 1661. Inflation, in beads.
  - Last line, the joke: "A shell company."

*The moment:* dig cowries out of the sand.

*Coin:* a cowrie.

### 4. Yap's stones

*Setting:* a lagoon village on Yap, stone discs leaning against house platforms, one stone under the water.
*Carved date:* none (the stones' history is not one date); the station sits here for its idea, not its year.

- **Rai stone** (tooltip: "Rai · stone money of Yap")
  - Limestone quarried on Palau, about 400 km away, and carried home by canoe and raft.
  - The biggest are over 3.5 m across. Many never move: when one changes hands, everybody knows, and the
    island's shared memory is the record.
  - Its worth depended on its size, its workmanship and its story, including how hard it was to bring home.
- **The stone in the lagoon** (tooltip: "Lost at sea · still counted")
  - William Furness, writing in 1910, told of a stone lost overboard on the way home. Its owners were still
    rich: everyone agreed it existed.
  - Milton Friedman later compared it to gold that never leaves a central bank's vault.
  - Last line: "A ledger everybody keeps. Ooga has heard of one of those."
- **O'Keefe's stones** (tooltip: "Iron-tool rai · worth less")
  - From 1872 the trader David O'Keefe shipped Yapese quarriers to Palau with iron tools, for copra.
  - Stones came easily, so they were valued less than the old ones cut by hand. Easy money buys less.

*The moment:* carry a stone on its pole with three other Oogas.

*Coin:* a little rai.

### 5. The first coins

*Setting:* a hillside mint in Lydia, a river of electrum-bearing sand, then an Athenian agora. *Carved date:*
**600 BCE** ("about").

- **Lion coin** (tooltip: "Lydian electrum · the first coins")
  - The first coins were struck in Lydia, in western Turkey, in the late 600s BCE, from electrum, a natural
    gold and silver mix.
  - A die with a lion's head sat on the anvil; a punch struck the back. Odd shapes, but strict weights.
  - Herodotus said the Lydians made the first gold and silver coins. Close: they were electrum.
- **Athenian owl** (tooltip: "The owl · Athens' silver")
  - Athens struck its silver four-drachma coins with an owl, from its own mines at Laurion.
  - Last line: "Sound money had a face. It was an owl."

*The moment:* strike a coin with the hammer.

*Coin:* a lion stamp.

### 6. Rome's debasement

*Setting:* a Roman mint and a forum, the emperor's bust over the furnace. *Carved date:* **301** (Diocletian's
edict).

- **The mint** (tooltip: "Roman mint · a little less silver each time")
  - The denarius started nearly pure silver.
  - In 64, after the Great Fire, Nero cut its silver and its weight.
  - Emperor after emperor did the same. By the 260s the main silver coin, the antoninianus, was about 5%
    silver, a copper coin with a silver wash.
- **The price edict** (tooltip: "Edict on Maximum Prices · 301")
  - Diocletian capped the prices of more than a thousand goods and wages across the empire.
  - It could not be enforced. A writer of the time, Lactantius, blamed it for bloodshed and empty markets.
  - Last line: "Prices were the symptom. The mint was the cause."

*The moment:* every poke at the mint melts the coin down with a little more copper in it, and the coin on the
counter darkens.

*Coin:* a denarius, bright.

### 7. Paper in China

*Setting:* a Song-dynasty printing house in Chengdu, then the Yuan court. *Carved date:* **1024**.

- **Printing block** (tooltip: "Jiaozi · the first government paper money")
  - Merchants in Sichuan issued paper notes first, because iron coins were too heavy to carry.
  - In 1024 the Song state took the notes over: the first government paper money.
- **Kublai's notes** (tooltip: "Yuan paper · Marco Polo wrote it down")
  - Marco Polo described the Great Khan's money: mulberry bark paper, stamped with the seal, forgery punished
    by death.
  - He wrote that the Khan could buy all the treasure in the world with it. The notes kept losing value, and
    new series replaced old ones.
  - Last line: "The first money printer was a printing block."

*The moment:* ink the block and press it; notes flutter off the table (the Jekyll printer's notes).

*Coin:* a Song cash coin with its square hole.

### 8. Tally sticks

*Setting:* the Exchequer's counting house in Westminster: a chequered table, stacks of split willow sticks, and a
stove. *Carved date:* **1834**.

- **Tally stick** (tooltip: "Exchequer tally · split willow receipt")
  - From the 1100s the English Exchequer recorded payments with notched willow sticks, split lengthwise: one half
    for each party. The halves only matched each other.
  - They were used until 1826.
- **The stove** (tooltip: "The stove · do not burn the tallies")
  - In October 1834 the old tallies were burned in the furnaces under the House of Lords.
  - The furnaces overheated. The fire destroyed most of the Palace of Westminster.
  - Last line: "Ooga burns old receipts in a barrel, outside."

*The moment:* poke the stove and the whole hall glows red, smoke curls under the door, and the station fills
with the sound of a crackling fire.

*Coin:* a notched tally.

### 9. Banks

*Setting:* an Amsterdam canal house and the Bank of England's counter. *Carved date:* **1694**.

- **Wisselbank** (tooltip: "Bank of Amsterdam · 1609")
  - Founded in 1609 to settle merchants' debts in good money, when fourteen mints and foreign coins made every
    payment a puzzle.
  - It kept close to full reserves and did not lend to the public.
- **Goldsmith's receipts** (tooltip: "Receipts · more of them than gold")
  - Goldsmiths' receipts for gold on deposit began to pass from hand to hand as money.
- **Bank of England** (tooltip: "Bank of England · 1694")
  - Founded in 1694 to lend the government £1.2 million for its war against France, in return for interest
    and the right to issue banknotes.
  - Last line: "Money for war. It will not be the last time."

*The moment:* a vault with a little gold and a tall stack of paper receipts on top of it; poke it and the stack
wobbles.

*Coin:* a goldsmith's receipt.

### 10. Newton and the bubbles

*Setting:* the Royal Mint in the Tower, and a Paris street full of share traders. *Carved date:* **1720**.

- **Newton's desk** (tooltip: "Isaac Newton · Master of the Mint")
  - Newton ran the Royal Mint from 1699 until his death in 1727.
  - In 1717 he set the gold guinea at 21 shillings. Silver went abroad, gold stayed, and Britain drifted onto a
    gold standard almost by accident.
- **Law's bank** (tooltip: "John Law · paper for France")
  - John Law's bank in Paris (1716), made a royal bank in 1718, printed notes in livres rather than in gold.
  - His Mississippi Company took on France's whole debt; notes bought shares, shares rose, more notes were
    printed. The word "millionaire" was born here.
  - In 1720 the bank ran out of metal to pay out, the shares went to nothing, and Law fled the country.
- **South Sea share** (tooltip: "South Sea Company · 1720")
  - London had its own bubble the same year.

*The moment:* a bubble machine: each poke blows the bubble bigger; the seventh pops it.

*Coin:* a gold guinea.

### 11. Revolution paper

*Setting:* a Continental Army camp in the snow, and a Paris print shop. *Carved date:* **1775**.

- **Continental note** (tooltip: "Continental · not worth one")
  - Congress paid for the War of Independence with paper Continentals from 1775.
  - Three to one against coin by late 1777, about forty to one by the end of 1779. By 1781 they no longer
    passed as money.
  - It left the phrase "not worth a Continental".
- **Assignat** (tooltip: "Assignat · France, 1789 to 1796")
  - Revolutionary France issued assignats against church lands it had seized.
  - More and more were printed until they were worthless by 1796.

*The moment:* paper notes blowing like snow round the camp fire.

*Coin:* a Continental note.

### 12. The gold standard

*Setting:* a bank vault of stacked bars, then the track runs into a tunnel toward Jekyll Island. *Carved date:*
**1900**.

- **Sovereign** (tooltip: "Gold sovereign · Britain on gold")
  - After the Napoleonic wars Britain put the pound formally on gold, convertible again from 1821.
  - By the 1870s most trading nations had joined: the classical gold standard, until the First World War in 1914.
- **Gold Standard Act** (tooltip: "United States · 1900")
  - The Gold Standard Act of 1900 put the dollar on gold alone.
  - Last line: "Next stop: Jekyll Island, 1910. Mind the gap."

*The moment:* the vault door swings open as the handcar rolls into the tunnel to Jekyll Island.

*Coin:* a sovereign.

## The cypherpunk islet (between Jekyll Island and the Mempool island)

*Setting:* a small islet at night, cables, an old server rack under a tarp, a mailing-list noticeboard, a mining
rig humming in a shed. Bridged from the genesis stone's side of Jekyll Island to the Mempool island.

- **Chaum's terminal** (tooltip: "DigiCash · ecash, 1989")
  - David Chaum's blind signatures let a bank sign money without seeing who would spend it.
  - His company DigiCash (founded 1989) launched ecash. Few banks joined, and it went bankrupt in 1998.
- **The list** (tooltip: "Cypherpunks · write code")
  - In 1992 Eric Hughes, Timothy May and John Gilmore started the Cypherpunks mailing list.
  - Hughes' manifesto, 9 March 1993: "Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age."
- **Hashcash** (tooltip: "Hashcash · 1997")
  - Adam Back's proof of work to stop spam: every email costs a little computing.
- **b-money and bit gold** (tooltip: "b-money · bit gold · 1998")
  - Wei Dai's b-money and Nick Szabo's bit gold described digital money no one controls. Neither was built.
- **RPOW** (tooltip: "Reusable proofs of work · Hal Finney, 2004")
  - Hal Finney built a working system on Hashcash, though a central server still checked it.
- **The white paper** (tooltip: "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System · 31 October 2008")
  - It cites both Hashcash and b-money. The bridge from here leads to the live chain.
- Easter egg: a terminal reading "Running bitcoin", after Hal Finney's post of January 2009.

*Carved date:* **2008**. The genesis stone (2009) stays the puzzle's last step.

## The dates puzzle

The Money Line keeps its own run of carved dates, poked in order: 1754 BCE, 1200 BCE, 600 BCE, 301, 1024, 1694,
1720, 1775, 1834, 1900. Stations 1 and 4 carry no date and never break a run. Finishing it opens the tunnel's
last door early (or another reward to settle), and the run on Jekyll Island (1910 to 2009) stays as it is. A full
run of both, in one visit, could be the reward for the coin set.

Open question: one puzzle across the whole route, or one per place as above. One per place keeps each scene's
state its own, which the scene contract prefers.

## The Conductor and the purse

**The Conductor** rides the handcar, pumping its lever, and talks: each leg's lines in turn as it rolls, the stop's
welcome with a hint toward its egg, a tip now and then while it waits, and a quip on the visitor's first cowrie, carry
and coin. All their lines live in `LINES` in `scene-moneyline.js`; the facts beyond the stations' own are:

- Many money cowries are *thought* to have come from the Maldives; historians assume it, archaeology has not yet
  shown it, so the line says "thought to".
- In 14th-century Mali, Ibn Battuta saw 1,150 cowries sold for one gold dinar.
- Cowries were still money in parts of West Africa around 1900, and in the 1800s thousands of tons of cheaper
  Zanzibar cowries poured in and devalued them.
- Croesus (reigned about 561 to 546 BCE) is credited with the first gold and silver coins of standard purity, a lion
  facing a bull on them.

**The purse** shows each era's money the visitor carries: cowries (dug), wampum beads (traded at the beach stall, one
cowrie for six at the Massachusetts rate of 1637), a share of a rai stone (carried), and Lydian electrum coins
(struck). Money can die in it: leaving the shells for Yap with wampum in the purse, the Conductor announces the 1661
repeal and the beads are struck through. The stalls take each era's money: a fish for a shell, bread for a coin.
Later stations add the money that rots on the way: the denarius losing silver, paper losing worth with each print,
Continentals at forty to one.

## Easter eggs

Hidden relics off the obvious path: behind something, up high, in the dark, or only there at night. Each is a
real, sourced detail. None is needed for the puzzle or the coin set; finding them is its own reward. Where the
record is shaky, the words say so.

**A golden banana in every place.** One small gold banana hides in each station, on the cypherpunk islet and on
Jekyll Island (14 in all). Finding them all is the hunt that runs through the whole route; the reward is cosmetic
(a golden banana for the locker), never a bonus to the pile.

## Myths we do not repeat

| The myth | What to say instead |
|---|---|
| Barter came first and money was invented to fix it | Anthropologists find credit and gifts first; barter mostly between strangers or after money fails (Humphrey; Graeber, *Debt*) |
| Roman soldiers were paid in salt | "Salary" does come from Latin *salarium*, linked to salt, but there is no ancient evidence soldiers were paid in salt |
| Newton said he could "calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people" | Attributed only, first printed decades later. Leave it out, or say "the story goes" |
| Tulip mania ruined the Dutch economy | Goldgar's archives show a small, short market of rich buyers; prices did soar and crash, and there was no depression |
| Kublai Khan invented paper money | China had used it for about two centuries already |
| Yap's stones were used for everyday shopping | They changed hands for big occasions; everyday goods used other things |
